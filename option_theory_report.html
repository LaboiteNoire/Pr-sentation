1   Question 1 — USD/VND Exchange Rate Options

We have 250 daily closing prices for the USD/VND exchange rate. Let $S_t$ denote the spot rate (VND per USD). Log-returns are $r_t = \ln(S_t/S_{t-1})$, giving $n=249$ observations.

1(a)  Price and Return Time Series

The spot rate moved from 25,980 to 26,355 VND/USD over the year, a modest VND depreciation of ~1.44% — consistent with the State Bank of Vietnam's managed float. The return series shows tight clustering typical of an actively managed currency.

Figure 1. USD/VND closing price over 250 trading days (top) and daily log-returns (bottom). The dashed green line marks the sample mean return. The narrow amplitude of the return series reflects the SBV's intervention bands.

1(b)  Annualised Parameters

Annualising with 250 trading days:

$$\hat{\mu} = \bar{r}\times 250 = 1.4389\%, \qquad \hat{\sigma} = s\times\sqrt{250} = 1.1415\%$$

The annual volatility of ~1.14% is far below freely floating currencies (typically 8–15%), confirming tight SBV management.

1(d)  Garman-Kohlhagen Pricing

The Garman-Kohlhagen (1983) model extends Black-Scholes to FX by treating the foreign rate as a continuous dividend yield. Under $\mathbb{Q}$:

$$C = S_0 e^{-r_f T}\Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-r_d T}\Phi(d_2), \quad P = Ke^{-r_d T}\Phi(-d_2) - S_0 e^{-r_f T}\Phi(-d_1)$$(2)
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K)+(r_d-r_f+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1-\sigma\sqrt{T}$$

Parameters: $r_d=4.5\%$ (VND), $r_f=4.3\%$ (USD), $K=26{,}200$, $T=2/12$ yr, $\sigma=1.14\%$.

1(f)  Jarrow-Rudd Binomial Tree (European)

The JR parameterisation sets $p=q=\frac{1}{2}$ and embeds the drift in the up/down factors:

$$u = e^{(r_d-r_f-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \quad d = e^{(r_d-r_f-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\Delta t - \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$$

Backward induction: $V_i^j = e^{-r_d\Delta t}\cdot\frac{1}{2}(V_{i+1}^j+V_{i+1}^{j+1})$.

Jarrow-Rudd tree (500 steps, European)

Call $= 167.6634$ VND  (GK error: $0.0101$ VND)

Put  $= \phantom{00}5.1007$ VND  (GK error: $0.0101$ VND)

1(g)  CRR Binomial Tree (American)

CRR uses $u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$, $d=1/u$, probability $p=(e^{(r_d-r_f)\Delta t}-d)/(u-d)$, and at each step takes:

$$V_i^j=\max\!\left(e^{-r_d\Delta t}[p\,V_{i+1}^j+(1-p)\,V_{i+1}^{j+1}],\;(S_i^j-K)^+\right)$$

CRR tree (500 steps, American)

American Call $= 167.67$ VND   Early exercise premium: $\approx 0$

American Put  $= \phantom{0}5.15$ VND   Early exercise premium: $+0.04$ VND

Early exercise on the call is never optimal. The put's small premium of 0.04 VND reflects the advantage of reinvesting the strike at the domestic rate $r_d$.

2(c)  Stochastic Differential Equations under the Real Measure $\mathbb{P}$

Individual GBMs

Under $\mathbb{P}$, each stock satisfies a geometric Brownian motion:

$$dS^{(i)}_t = \mu_i\,S^{(i)}_t\,dt + \sigma_i\,S^{(i)}_t\,dW^{(i)}_t, \qquad i\in\{1,2\}$$(3)

where $W^{(1)},W^{(2)}$ are $\mathbb{P}$-Brownian motions with $d\langle W^{(1)},W^{(2)}\rangle_t = \rho\,dt$.

Cholesky Decomposition

Using independent BMs $W^{(1)}\perp W^{(3)}$, the correlation is factored as:

$$W^{(2)}_t = \rho\,W^{(1)}_t + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,W^{(3)}_t$$(4)

Verification: $\text{Var}[W^{(2)}_t]=t$ ✓, $d\langle W^{(1)},W^{(2)}\rangle_t=\rho\,dt$ ✓. This separates META's risk into a systematic component shared with NVDA (via $W^{(1)}$) and an idiosyncratic component (via $W^{(3)}$):

$$dS^{(2)}_t = \mu_2 S^{(2)}_t\,dt + \sigma_2 S^{(2)}_t\!\left[\rho\,dW^{(1)}_t + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,dW^{(3)}_t\right]$$(5)

Closed-Form Solutions via Itô's Lemma

Applying Itô's lemma to $f(S)=\ln S$:

$$S^{(i)}_T = S^{(i)}_0\exp\!\left[\left(\mu_i-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_i^2\right)T + \sigma_i W^{(i)}_T\right]$$(6)

Fitted SDEs under $\mathbb{P}$

$dS^{(1)} = 0.5584\,S^{(1)}\,dt + 0.2558\,S^{(1)}\,dW^{(1)}$  (NVDA)

$dS^{(2)} = 0.0189\,S^{(2)}\,dt + 0.5129\,S^{(2)}[0.5843\,dW^{(1)} + 0.8117\,dW^{(3)}]$  (META)

2(d)  Distribution of $S^{(1)}_T/S^{(2)}_T$ under the $S^{(2)}$-Numeraire Measure $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$

Step 1 — Risk-Neutral Dynamics under $\mathbb{Q}$

Under $\mathbb{Q}$ (money-market numeraire $B_t=e^{rt}$), Girsanov's theorem replaces each drift with $r$:

$$dS^{(i)}_t = r\,S^{(i)}_t\,dt + \sigma_i\,S^{(i)}_t\,d\widetilde{W}^{(i)}_t, \quad d\langle\widetilde{W}^{(1)},\widetilde{W}^{(2)}\rangle_t=\rho\,dt$$

Step 2 — Itô Formula for the Ratio $X_t = S^{(1)}_t/S^{(2)}_t$

Applying the Itô product rule to $X_t = S^{(1)}_t\cdot(S^{(2)}_t)^{-1}$, and collecting the Itô-on-$1/S^{(2)}$ term and the cross-variation term:

Itô on $1/S^{(2)}$ $$d\!\left(\tfrac{1}{S^{(2)}_t}\right) = \tfrac{1}{S^{(2)}_t}\!\left[(\sigma_2^2-r)\,dt - \sigma_2\,d\widetilde{W}^{(2)}_t\right]$$
Product rule & cross-variation $$\frac{dX_t}{X_t} = (\sigma_2^2-\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2)\,dt + \sigma_1\,d\widetilde{W}^{(1)}_t - \sigma_2\,d\widetilde{W}^{(2)}_t$$

Step 3 — Change of Numeraire to $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$

By the change-of-numeraire theorem (Geman, El Karoui & Rochet, 1995), switching from $\mathbb{Q}$ to $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$ shifts each BM by its covariance with $\ln S^{(2)}$. The $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$-BMs are:

$$\widehat{W}^{(2)}_t = \widetilde{W}^{(2)}_t - \sigma_2\,t, \qquad \widehat{W}^{(1)}_t = \widetilde{W}^{(1)}_t - \rho\sigma_2\,t$$

Substituting into the SDE for $X_t$, the drift cancels exactly:

SDE for $X_t$ under $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$ — drift vanishes $$\frac{dX_t}{X_t} = \sigma_1\,d\widehat{W}^{(1)}_t - \sigma_2\,d\widehat{W}^{(2)}_t$$

Under $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$, the ratio $X_t=S^{(1)}_t/S^{(2)}_t$ is a martingale. This is the fundamental property that makes Margrabe's formula work without a risk-free rate.

Step 4 — Log-Normal Distribution

The combined volatility $\sigma_X=\sqrt{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}$ satisfies:

$$d(\ln X_t) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_X^2\,dt + \sigma_X\,d\widehat{W}_t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\!\left(\frac{X_T}{X_0}\right)\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_X^2 T,\;\sigma_X^2 T\right) \text{ under }\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$$(7)

Numerical values

$\sigma_X=\sqrt{0.2558^2+0.5129^2-2(0.5843)(0.2558)(0.5129)}=41.85\%$

$X_0=198.45/608.75=0.3260$

$\ln(X_T)\sim\mathcal{N}(-1.1428,\;0.0438)$ under $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$

2(e)  Margrabe Exchange Option Formula

The no-arbitrage price is $V_0=e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\max(Q_2 S^{(2)}_T-Q_1 S^{(1)}_T,0)]$. Factoring $S^{(2)}_T$ and applying the change of numeraire:

$$V_0 = S^{(2)}_0\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}}\!\left[\max(Q_2 - Q_1 X_T,\,0)\right]$$

Since $Q_1 X_T$ is log-normal under $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$, the expectation evaluates by the Black-Scholes integral to:

$$\boxed{V_0 = F_2\,\Phi(d_1) - F_1\,\Phi(d_2)}$$(8)
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(F_2/F_1)+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_X^2 T}{\sigma_X\sqrt{T}}, \quad d_2=d_1-\sigma_X\sqrt{T}, \quad F_i=Q_i S^{(i)}_0$$

This is Margrabe's (1978) formula. The risk-free rate $r$ does not appear — the price depends only on the joint log-normality of the two assets under $\mathbb{Q}^{(2)}$. When $Q_1=1$ and $S^{(2)}\equiv K$ (constant), the formula reduces to the standard Black-Scholes call.

Margrabe results ($Q_1=3$, $Q_2=1$, $T=3/12$ yr)

$F_1=3\times198.45=595.35$, $F_2=1\times608.75=608.75$

$\sigma_X=41.85\%$, $d_1=0.2110$, $d_2=0.0017$

Exchange option price $V_0 = \$57.15$

2(f)  Option Price as a Function of Correlation

From $\sigma_X^2=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2-2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$, we get $\partial\sigma_X^2/\partial\rho=-2\sigma_1\sigma_2<0$. Since the option price has positive vega, it is strictly decreasing in $\rho$.

2(h)  Delta Hedging: Theory and Simulation

Setup

The trader sold the option at a 10% mark-up: Premium $=\$57.15\times1.10=\$62.87$. A daily delta-neutral hedge is maintained over 63 trading steps.

Margrabe Deltas

Differentiating equation (8):

$$\Delta^{(2)}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial S^{(2)}}=Q_2\,\Phi(d_1)>0\quad\text{(long META)}$$(9)
$$\Delta^{(1)}=\frac{\partial V}{\partial S^{(1)}}=-Q_1\,\Phi(d_2)<0\quad\text{(short NVDA)}$$(10)

The trader who sold the option must hold long META and short NVDA to remain delta-neutral.

Self-Financing Rebalancing

At each daily step the self-financing condition gives:

$$C_{k+1}=C_k\,e^{r\Delta t}-(\Delta^{(1)}_{k+1}-\Delta^{(1)}_k)S^{(1)}_{k+1}-(\Delta^{(2)}_{k+1}-\Delta^{(2)}_k)S^{(2)}_{k+1}$$

At expiry: $\text{P\&L}=C_{63}+\Delta^{(1)}_{63}S^{(1)}_T+\Delta^{(2)}_{63}S^{(2)}_T-\text{Payoff}_T$. Paths are simulated under $\mathbb{P}$ (with $\mu_1,\mu_2$) to reflect actual hedge performance.

Figure 6. (Left) P&L distribution from 10,000 hedge simulations; the green line is the mean P&L, the red line is $-\text{VaR}_{99\%}$. (Right) Loss distribution with the 99% VaR (navy) and 99% ES (purple) marked.

Interpretation. The mean P&L of $5.57 is close to the theoretical mark-up of $\$5.72$; the gap reflects discrete rebalancing costs and real-world drift. The standard deviation of $5.76 is driven by gamma risk: the non-linearity of the option means daily rebalancing leaves a residual. The 99% VaR of $10.50 exceeds the mean P&L, so in the worst 1% of scenarios the mark-up is insufficient to cover hedging losses. The 99% ES of $15.07 (average loss in those tail scenarios) matters for economic capital. These results show why exchange-option writers typically demand larger mark-ups, or complement delta hedging with gamma and vega hedging using vanilla options.
